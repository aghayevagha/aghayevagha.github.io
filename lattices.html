Lattice-Based Cryptography: A Student's Roadmap

From mathematical foundations to research-level post-quantum cryptography

Why Lattice Cryptography?

Modern cryptography is built on mathematically hard problems, which are believed to be infeasible to solve efficiently with today’s supercomputers. However, Peter Shor introduced an efficient quantum algorithm for integer factorization and discrete logarithms. Governments and large companies are investing billions of dollars in scalable quantum computers, which may create serious security challenges for contemporary systems.

This motivates Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC), cryptographic constructions designed to be secure against both classical and quantum attacks. One of the most prominent candidates for PQC is lattice-based cryptography.

Lattices are grid-like structures spanning an n-dimensional space and rely heavily on efficient matrix and vector computations. Their most attractive property is worst-case security: solving certain average-case lattice problems is as hard as solving related problems in the worst case.

Phase 1 — Foundations Beginner

From my experience, a strong background in linear algebra is essential. You should be comfortable with vector spaces, bases, and matrix operations.

The first book to review is Section 7 of Introduction to Mathematical Cryptography . It introduces lattices from basic definitions and presents the Shortest Vector Problem (SVP) and the Closest Vector Problem (CVP).

SVP is one of the most well-studied NP-hard problems and forms the foundation for many security reductions. Many modern problems, such as LWE and SIS, reduce to approximate versions of SVP.

The notions of good and bad bases are explained clearly. You will see their first major application in the GGH signature scheme based on CVP. This gives intuition about decoding using a “nice” basis and how it serves as secret information.

You will also encounter algebraic lattices such as NTRU lattices — one of the earliest structured lattice families. Although early constructions were broken, the underlying problems became central to modern efficient cryptographic schemes, and even went to NIST's selection.

Lattice reduction is another fundamental topic. Given a basis, the goal is to make vectors shorter and more orthogonal. The LLL algorithm is the first step, although its approximation factor for SVP is exponential. LLL has also been used to attack early lattice schemes and knapsack-based cryptosystems.

For modern lattice cryptanalysis with concrete applications and code, see: A Gentle Tutorial for Lattice-Based Cryptanalysis .

Phase 2 — Modern Hardness Assumptions Intermediate

After building foundations, you can move to advanced cryptanalytic techniques and modern hardness assumptions such as Learning With Errors (LWE) and Short Integer Solution (SIS). Cryptographic constructions often require average-case hardness, meaning random instances of the problem is hard, hence it is easy to create crypto from them. Moreover, a central theme here is worst-case to average-case reductions. These reductions are what make lattice-based cryptography theoretically compelling. It i shown that if you can solve LWE and SIS on average, you can also solve (approximate) worst-case hard problems such as SVP that you studied on previous sections.

LWE and SIS are extremely versatile and are used in identity-based encryption (IBE), attribute-based encryption (ABE), homomorphic encryption, functional encryption, and many other constructions. A natural next step is Peikert’s survey: A Decade of Lattice Cryptography .

🔬 Phase 3 — Advanced Constructions Advanced

For deeper understanding, security reductions, and concrete modern constructions from lattices, consult lecture notes by experts:

  • Regev’s Notes
  • Peikert’s Notes
  • Micciancio’s Notes

My personal favorite is Vaikuntanathan’s Lecture Notes , where topics such as gadget-based trapdoors, lattice signatures, IBE, ABE, FHE, functional encryption, and Gaussian sampling are presented clearly. For the original trapdoor sampling construction, see the seminal GPV paper.

Phase 4 — Structured Lattices & Concrete Security Research

The next step is algebraically structured lattices such as ideal and module lattices. These provide compact keys and use of faster algorithms using FFT-like techniques, and are widely used in practice. However, assessing the precise security of structured lattices remains an active research area. You may start with the Alfred Menezes Lecture Series on lattices, where he also introduces algebraic lattices.

BKZ reduction is an advanced reduction algorithm, which applies reduction on blocks of vectors (this is 2 in LLL). It is often used to approximate the hardness of lattice problems: many problems are reduced to SVP problem, and then solved with BKZ algorithm.

Finally, The LIP problem has also gained attention due to strong decoding properties and its role in the NIST finalist HAWK. See: Ducas’s Paper for more details.
